All values are in today's money. The band widens with age because old cars are harder to price — a 12-year-old estimate is a guess, not a quote.

The line is a depreciation rule of thumb, not a market dataset. It applies a retention curve to the price the car was new, then adjusts that for mileage and history.

Age of car Value kept per year
Year 1 82%
Years 2–3 88%
Years 4–6 90%
Years 7–10 91%
11 years and older 93%

Compounding those rates leaves about 38% of the original price at 8 years and 15% at 20. Electric cars use 98% of each rate; hybrids 100.5%.

value(age) = priceWhenNew × retention(age) × mileageFactor × condition × history × (1 + marketAdjust) × inflationAdjust(age)
  • priceWhenNew is your optional override. Otherwise it is today's new price divided by (1 + inflation)age — that is why a car is never valued against a sticker it was never sold at.
  • mileageFactor is 1 − 3.5% per 10,000 km above average (age × typical km per year), floored at 55%. Below average it is 1 + 2.8% per 10,000 km, capped at 125%.
  • inflationAdjust scales points before today so the whole curve reads in today's money.
  • Band is ±(5.5% + 1.3% per year), capped at ±20%.

Not fitted to transaction data: the brackets approximate the shape of published residual-value curves, and the condition and history multipliers are judgement calls. Five comparable local listings will beat this every time.

Where this estimate falls down

  • Three inputs are not enough for a valuation. Age, distance and a new price get you within maybe ±15% on a common car. Two cars with identical numbers can differ by thousands on history, spec and local supply. This page is a sanity check, not an appraisal.
  • "Current new price" is the weakest input. If the new price rose 25% while this car sat in a driveway, valuing against today's sticker overstates it. The model back-inflates the original price, and you can override it — but if the model was replaced or re-specced, that number is fiction.
  • Odometer without service history is a half-truth. A 200,000 km highway car with full records is often a better buy than a 90,000 km city car with none. The model prices the history; only an inspection confirms it.
  • Not modelled at all: reputation for reliability, rare trims and colours, modifications, import duties, remaining warranty, tyre and brake life, EV battery state of health, supply shortages, and how well you negotiate.
  • What actually settles a price: five comparable local listings with similar age, odometer and spec. If this page and the market disagree, trust the market and adjust your assumptions.
